Net load is the load plan with wind and solar taken out. It is what dispatchable plant actually has to cover, and it is the curve that decides which hours are tight.

Two series are published while the day-ahead market is still open: the load plan and the wind forecast. EPİAŞ publishes no solar forecast, so solar is shaped from the mean realised profile of the seven preceding days. Bands are quintiles of the last thirty days rather than of the target day, so a flat day reads as flat instead of being rescaled to look dramatic.

Why this curve and not demand

Demand peaks in the early afternoon in Türkiye through the summer. Net load usually does not: solar is at its strongest in exactly those hours, so the system is at its most comfortable when the meter reads highest. The tight hours arrive after sunset, when solar leaves in about an hour and demand is still near its plateau.

That gap is the whole scheduling problem. On a typical August day the load plan peaks around 14:00 while net load peaks around 20:00, and between those two points the system has to climb roughly 2,400 MWh every hour for eight hours. Reading demand alone hides that climb entirely.

Method

NET LOAD
Load plan minus wind forecast minus solar.
SOLAR
No EPİAŞ forecast exists. Shaped from the mean realised profile of the seven preceding days, which includes distributed rooftop output derived as consumption minus metered generation.
BANDS
Quintiles of the last thirty days, not of the target day.
SCOPE
Descriptive analysis of published data. Not advice, not a forecast, not a trading recommendation.
Türkiye · daily · ex-post check

Actual vs outlook

Every net load outlook is published before the day-ahead price exists. The next day the same hours can be checked against what actually cleared, which is the only honest way to know whether the method is worth anything.

The outlook never forecasts price. What it claims is structural: which hours sit at the bottom of the range, which sit at the top, and where the ramp runs. Those claims either hold or they do not.

13 August 2026, checked against outturn

The first outlook to be verified after publication. All four structural claims held.

What a failed check would look like

The method has three ways to be wrong, and each points somewhere different. If the deep window lands in the right hours but the price does not follow, the bid stack is doing the work rather than the fundamentals. If the window itself lands in the wrong hours, the solar shaping is off, which is the weakest input because no forecast is published. If the evening block is right but the ramp is not, the wind forecast missed.

Recording which of the three fails is more useful than recording whether the day was right. A single check proves nothing; a run of them tells you which input to fix.

Weekly review

Seven days is the shortest window where the difference between weather and structure starts to show.

In preparation The weekly view will carry three things the daily posts cannot: how the trough and the peak moved across the week rather than day to day, how much of the week's balancing bill fell on which hours, and whether the hours that ran short kept running short. A single day tells you the weather. A week starts to tell you the market.

Monthly review

Türkiye · yearly

Yearly review

Structural change is only visible at a year, and it is the only horizon where an investment decision lives.

In preparation The yearly view will track what changed rather than what happened: how the midday trough deepened as distributed solar grew, how many hours cleared below the penalty threshold, how the evening ramp steepened, and where the flexibility gap sits. These are the numbers that decide whether storage is worth building, and none of them can be read from a single day.

Great Britain · monthly

Wind Imbalance Scorecard

Deviation from final physical notification, imbalance cost per MWh, and directional accuracy for every transmission-connected wind BMU in Great Britain.

Built from Elexon Insights open data. The GB fleet publishes physical notifications and metered volumes per unit, which makes something possible that cannot be done in Türkiye: ranking generators by how well they manage their own imbalance, and separating the size of a forecast error from its direction.

First report: September 2026 B1610 metered volumes publish five days after delivery, so the August scorecard runs in the second week of September.

Why direction matters more than accuracy

GB settles imbalance at a single price. A generator that comes in short when the system is short pays that price; a generator that comes in short when the system is long is paid. The same 2% deviation can therefore cost several times more for one unit than another, and the ranking by deviation size is not the ranking by cost.

Türkiye settles at a dual price, where a deviation is valued at whichever of the day-ahead and balancing prices is worse for the generator. Direction carries no reward there, only the size of the error matters. That is the single largest difference between managing a portfolio on each side, and it is why the GB scorecard reports a column that has no Turkish equivalent.

Method

DEVIATION
Metered volume minus FPN energy, per settlement period. FPN is a MW level, so energy is (levelFrom + levelTo) ÷ 2 × 0.5 h.
COST
Deviation × system price, summed over the month.
DIRECTION
A deviation is helpful when its sign is opposite to net imbalance volume.
SCOPE
Transmission-connected BMUs with fuel type WIND. Embedded wind sits outside the balancing mechanism. Units, not sites — Hornsea 2 reports as three BMUs.
TIMING
FPN is submitted at gate closure, one hour before delivery, so a low deviation reflects notification accuracy at gate closure rather than day-ahead forecast skill.
RUN TYPE
Interim settlement. Final reconciliation can move these figures.
Great Britain · market structure

Two markets, four differences

GB and Türkiye run the same sequence — day-ahead, intraday, balancing — but the mechanics diverge in ways that change what an imbalance actually costs.

Written for people who know one of these markets well and need the other quickly. Times are from the exchanges' own published schedules.

Where the day is decided

GB closes its day-ahead auctions almost three hours earlier than Türkiye, which means a British generator commits to a position while its weather forecast is that much less mature.

Four structural differences

TürkiyeGreat Britain
Settlement period1 hour, 24 per day30 minutes, 48 per day
Day-ahead venuesOne exchange, one priceTwo exchanges, two prices
Imbalance pricingDual price: min and max of MCP and SMPSingle price, so direction decides gain or loss
Price cap4,500 TL/MWh ceilingNo equivalent ceiling; negative prices occur

GB did not move to 15-minute market time units in October 2025. That change covered eleven coupled markets and excluded Great Britain and Switzerland, so the half-hourly settlement period stays.

Why the last two rows matter most

Under dual pricing every deviation is a loss, so the objective is simply to be accurate. Under a single price, being wrong in the direction the system needs is paid, so the objective changes: a portfolio can be deliberately short into a short system and earn from it. The same forecast error produces a penalty in one market and a payment in the other.

The price cap works the same way in reverse. A ceiling means scarcity stops being priced past a point, so the day-ahead market can clear at the cap for hours while real time settles far lower, which is exactly what happened in Türkiye on 26 August. Without a ceiling, GB lets the price go where the last accepted offer sits, and lets it go negative when there is too much.
